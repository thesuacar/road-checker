\section{Data Preparation}
\label{sec:data-preparation}


\textbf{Processing pipeline.}
The three smartphone sensor streams were first aligned using their timestamps
and consolidated at recording level. To reduce sensitivity to phone
orientation, the analysis then transformed the original sensor axes into
physically interpretable signals.

The magnitude of the gravity vector was computed and used to define the local
vertical direction. Calibrated dynamic acceleration was projected onto this
gravity direction to obtain \texttt{vertical\_dyn\_acc}, representing bicycle
motion approximately normal to the road surface. The Euclidean magnitude of
the three gyroscope axes was calculated as \texttt{gyroscope\_mag}, while
vertical jerk was obtained from the discrete time derivative of vertical
dynamic acceleration.

The gyroscope magnitude was also used to distinguish stopped, transitional
and active-riding periods. A smoothed gyroscope RMS envelope was compared
with an empirically investigated motion threshold. Samples surrounding
start/stop transitions were excluded using a 2~s transition buffer, and only
windows classified as \textit{In Movement} were retained for feature
extraction. This avoids allowing stationary periods to dominate the
Smooth class.

\textbf{Frequency analysis and windowing.}
The physics experiment in Section \ref{sec:physics-eda} indicated that pedalling motion remained
below approximately 2~Hz, while plausible road-excitation frequencies covered
a substantially higher range. For the measured combinations of bicycle speed
and terrain spacing, the estimated road excitation extended approximately
from 5 to 51~Hz.

Considering both this physical argument and the approximately 100~Hz sensor
sampling frequency, a fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter from
4--46~Hz was applied to vertical dynamic acceleration. The upper limit remains
below the 50~Hz Nyquist frequency of the retained recordings. The filter is
used as an analysis band rather than as an assumption that every component
inside 4--46~Hz is necessarily caused by road roughness.

Welch power spectral density estimates were used to examine the terrain
classes before modelling. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:physics-informed},
Bumpy windows exhibit consistently greater spectral energy over much of the
candidate road-vibration region. The median 4--46~Hz RMS magnitude was
1.205~m/s$^2$ for Bumpy windows compared with 0.673~m/s$^2$ for Smooth
windows, corresponding to an approximately $1.79\times$ ratio.

Together with the independent pedalling experiment in
Section~\ref{sec:physics-eda}, this supported use of the 4--46~Hz band for
analysis. 

Once again, the band should nevertheless be interpreted as a physically
motivated analysis region rather than evidence that all energy within it is
road-generated.

The state-gated riding data (that was classified as \textit{In Movement}) were divided into 2~s windows with 50\% overlap,
resulting in 4246 valid windows. Because the current filtering implementation uses non-causal processing,
the present pipeline should be regarded as an offline analysis pipeline. If we consider a real-time implementation,
the classifier would need to be triggered by the state-gating DSP-based algorithm. Besides, it's worth noting that the
2~s windows with 50\% overlap additionally introduce temporal dependence between neighbouring feature vectors.

\textbf{Features and reduction.}
Twelve candidate features were engineered from each valid window:
acceleration RMS, standard deviation and peak-to-peak amplitude;
gyroscope RMS; vertical-jerk RMS; RMS vibration in the 4--46~Hz road
band; spectral energy in the 0--3, 3--10, 10--20 and 20--40~Hz bands;
total spectral energy in 4--46~Hz; and a high-to-low-frequency energy
ratio.

Feature redundancy and mutual information with the terrain label were
analysed using the \textbf{training partition only}. The higher-frequency and 
vibration-related features carried most of the information about the terrain label.
The strongest mutual-information scores were obtained for
20--40~Hz spectral energy ($MI=0.312$), jerk RMS ($0.289$),
4--46~Hz road-band RMS ($0.252$), and acceleration peak-to-peak
amplitude ($0.230$).

Several features nevertheless represented closely related physical behaviour.
Acceleration RMS and standard deviation were effectively collinear, while
jerk RMS and road-band RMS showed $|r|=0.954$. Because several of the strongest
features were also highly correlated, we did not simply keep the features with
the highest individual scores. Instead, we compared three feature sets during modelling.

Three candidate representations were evaluated:
\texttt{full\_12}, containing all twelve features;
\texttt{compact\_physics\_5}, containing five physically motivated features;
and \texttt{compact\_ratio\_4}, containing four features including the
high/low spectral-energy ratio.

Interestingly, Logistic Regression still performed best with all 12 features,
despite the redundancy between some of them. k-NN behaved differently and performed
best with the reduced \texttt{compact\_physics\_5} set (see figure \ref{fig:appendix-supervised-cm}).
