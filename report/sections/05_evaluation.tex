\section{Evaluation} \label{sec:evaluation}
\textbf{Selection criterion.}
Bumpy was consistently treated as the positive class. Model development was
conducted exclusively on the training partition, with $F_{0.5}$ used as the
primary selection criterion because false Bumpy alarms are considered more
costly than missed detections, while non-zero recall remains necessary for a
useful classifier.

All five grouped validation folds contained examples of both classes, so
class-dependent metrics including ROC--AUC were mathematically defined for
each fold. Precision, recall, specificity, $F_1$, balanced accuracy,
ROC--AUC and average precision were nevertheless interpreted as supporting
diagnostics rather than independently optimised objectives.

\textbf{Supervised comparison.}
Each of the four supervised families was evaluated using all three candidate
feature representations under the same frozen grouped cross-validation
scheme. Hyperparameters were fitted inside the grouped folds, after which
training-only out-of-fold probabilities were used to determine a decision
threshold for each candidate.

The strongest configuration from each model family achieved training
out-of-fold $F_{0.5}$ values of 0.855 for Logistic Regression,
0.848 for k-NN, 0.847 for Random Forest and 0.828 for Decision Tree.
The differences between the first three approaches were therefore relatively
small, but Logistic Regression provided the highest validation criterion.

The resulting final specification was consequently frozen as Logistic
Regression using the complete \texttt{full\_12} feature set, RobustScaler,
$C=0.1$, and a Bumpy probability threshold of $0.68$. Both the model
specification and the threshold were fixed before accessing the held-out test
recordings.

\textbf{Final model rationale.}
Logistic Regression was selected not only because it achieved the highest
training-only OOF $F_{0.5}$, but also because its characteristics are well
matched to the intended application. The problem uses a compact engineered
feature representation rather than raw high-dimensional signals, making a
regularised linear classifier appropriate for the available dataset size.
Its coefficients and probability outputs are also more directly interpretable
than those of the ensemble alternative, while inference requires only a small
number of arithmetic operations per window. This makes the model comparatively
lightweight for future mobile or edge deployment. Random Forest and k-NN were very
close in validation performance, so there was little reason to choose the more complex
alternative over Logistic Regression. The latter is also easier to interpret and lightweight
enough for a future mobile or edge implementation.

\textbf{Clustering comparison.}
The unsupervised analysis produced a different result depending on whether
internal cluster compactness or agreement with the known terrain classes was
considered. Agglomerative Clustering obtained the highest Silhouette score
($0.927$), but its ARI and NMI were only approximately $0.001$, indicating
that its very well-separated clusters did not correspond meaningfully to the
Smooth/Bumpy distinction.

Similarly, GMM achieved a Silhouette score of $0.855$ but only
ARI $=0.010$ and NMI $=0.015$. K-Means showed somewhat greater correspondence
with the terrain labels (ARI $=0.045$, NMI $=0.067$), while DBSCAN produced
the highest diagnostic agreement among the clustering approaches
(ARI $=0.060$, NMI $=0.069$) and classified approximately 7\% of the
training observations as noise.

The high Silhouette scores show that there is clear structure in the engineered
feature space, but the very low ARI and NMI values show that this structure does 
not correspond well to Smooth versus Bumpy terrain. In other words, a high Silhouette
score does not necessarily mean that the clusters represent the terrain classes we care about.
Furthermore, Agglomerative Clustering does not naturally provide an
out-of-sample prediction rule, while standard DBSCAN cannot directly assign
arbitrary future observations without an additional deployment mechanism.

\textbf{Frozen hold-out evaluation.}
After all model, feature and threshold decisions had been frozen, the four
independent held-out recordings were evaluated once.

The final Logistic Regression classifier obtained accuracy $=0.565$,
Bumpy precision $=0.964$, recall $=0.211$, specificity $=0.991$,
$F_{0.5}=0.563$, $F_1=0.347$, balanced accuracy $=0.601$,
ROC--AUC $=0.947$, and average precision $=0.942$.

Figure~\ref{fig:heldout-cm} makes the selected operating trade-off explicit:
only 4 of 424 Smooth windows were falsely flagged as Bumpy, while 403 of
511 Bumpy windows were missed. The classifier therefore achieved the intended
low false-positive behaviour, but at the cost of substantial false negatives.

The reduction from OOF $F_{0.5}=0.855$ to held-out $F_{0.5}=0.563$
also shows that grouped training validation underestimated the difficulty of
generalising to some unseen recordings.

This weakness was not uniform across the hold-out set. Raw001 classified all
46 Smooth windows correctly, while Raw008 achieved 98.9\% accuracy. The two
Bumpy recordings behaved very differently: Raw022 achieved 87.5\% accuracy
with mean Bumpy probability $0.868$, whereas Raw011 achieved only 11.6\%
accuracy with mean probability $0.414$.

This suggests recording- or condition-dependent distribution shift rather
than a global inability to rank rough terrain. The high ROC--AUC together
with low thresholded recall also indicates that the fixed $0.68$ threshold
was conservative on the held-out distribution. It should not, however, be
retrospectively tuned using this test set.

\textbf{Supervised versus unsupervised behaviour.}
Supervised learning explicitly tries to separate the known Smooth and Bumpy
classes, whereas clustering searches for structure without knowing those labels.
Supervised learning directly optimises separation of the predefined Smooth and Bumpy
classes, whereas clustering searches for structure in feature space without
assuming that terrain condition is the dominant source of variation.

For this task, supervised learning is therefore preferable because the target
classes are explicitly known and deployment requires direct classification.
The unsupervised models remain useful as exploratory tools for revealing latent
structure, identifying atypical observations and testing whether the known
terrain labels correspond to natural groupings. The weak correspondence
between clusters and terrain labels suggests that other factors---such as
rider, recording session, cycling speed, phone orientation or vibration
intensity---also contribute substantially to the structure of the feature
space.
