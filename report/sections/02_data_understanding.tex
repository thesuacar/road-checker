\section{Data Understanding}
\label{sec:data-understanding}

\subsection{Acquisition Protocol}

\textbf{Acquisition protocol.}
Smartphone sensor data were collected while participants cycled over road
segments labelled as either \textit{Smooth} or \textit{Bumpy}. The smartphone
was placed in the rider's back pocket to provide a reasonably consistent
measurement position while limiting direct displacement caused by leg
movement. The initial collection plan targeted at least five recordings of each terrain
type per participant. Three participants originally collected data,
of whom two remained after quality screening.

Three sensor streams were retained for the analysis: calibrated
accelerometer, gravity and calibrated gyroscope measurements, each containing
three spatial axes. Participant identities were replaced by anonymous numeric
codes, while raw files followed the naming convention
\texttt{RawXXX\_Terrain.zip}. Raw packages were stored locally before
processing rather than being retrieved automatically from external storage.

\textbf{Quality screening.}
The effective sampling frequency of every sensor stream was estimated from
the mean interval between successive timestamps. Most usable recordings were
measured close to the intended 100~Hz sampling rate. Recordings originating
from participant~\#03 were instead sampled at approximately 62~Hz.

At 62~Hz the corresponding Nyquist frequency is only approximately
31~Hz. This is incompatible with the later frequency-domain analysis
(as can be seen in figure \ref{fig:physics-informed}), which uses information
extending to 46~Hz. Upsampling these recordings would increase the sample count but 
could not reconstruct frequency content that was never measured. Participant~\#03 was
therefore excluded rather than mixing measurements with fundamentally
different observable bandwidths. To compensate for the reduced number of participants,
more data was collected as per appendix \ref{app:data-collection-plan}.

After this quality-control step, 26 recordings were retained, corresponding
to approximately 4789~s (79.8~min) of sensor data. This exclusion improves
measurement consistency but reduces participant diversity: all subsequent
modelling results are based on only two retained participants and should
therefore not be interpreted as demonstrating population-level
generalisation.

\textbf{Available independent units.}
After operational-state filtering and window extraction, the retained data
produced 4246 valid riding windows, comprising 2431 Smooth and 1815 Bumpy
windows. These windows are not statistically independent observations because
neighbouring windows overlap and several recordings originated from the same
continuous acquisition session.

In particular, Raw031--Raw037 were segmented from one original annotated 
recording and were therefore represented by the shared group
\texttt{AnnotatedSession01}. Raw038--Raw041 similarly originated from a
second recording and were represented by \texttt{AnnotatedSession02}.
These parent-session identifiers were subsequently used during dataset
partitioning and cross-validation to prevent segments from the same original
ride appearing on opposite sides of a validation split.

The measured sampling frequencies are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:sampling-frequencies}. The clear
 hardware-dependent difference supports exclusion of participant~\#03 rather than artificial resampling,
 which could not recover unobserved spectral content above its Nyquist limit.

\subsection{Physics-Informed Exploratory Analysis}
\label{sec:physics-eda}

Before defining the frequency-domain features, an exploratory experiment was
performed to investigate whether bicycle-induced road vibration could be
separated from low-frequency rider motion.

Road excitation was approximated from bicycle speed $v$ and the characteristic
spacing $\lambda$ between surface irregularities:

\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{road}} \approx \frac{v}{\lambda}.
\end{equation}

For a measured irregularity spacing of $\lambda=100$~mm, repeated 10~m runs
produced estimated excitation frequencies of 25.6~Hz, 39.7~Hz and 51.2~Hz
for slow, medium and fast cycling respectively. Considering the largest
observed spacing ($\lambda\approx500$~mm) together with the slowest measured
speed ($v\approx2.5$~m/s) gives an approximate lower road-excitation bound of
5~Hz.

Rider-motion frequency was independently estimated by manually counting
complete pedalling cycles from video recordings:

\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{pedal}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{cycles}}}{t}.
\end{equation}

Even during intentionally fast cycling, measured pedalling frequencies ranged
from approximately 1.20 to 1.53~Hz (72--92~RPM), remaining below 2~Hz.
The experiment therefore indicated a useful separation between dominant
pedalling motion and plausible road-induced vibration. This observation
motivated treating the low-frequency region as rider-motion dominated and
investigating a higher-frequency road-vibration band during subsequent
signal processing.
